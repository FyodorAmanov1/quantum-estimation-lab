# QE03: Risk under non-Gaussian returns

## Question

At fixed fitted parameters, contracts and pricing inputs, how sensitive are
the existing four-asset option book's 99% VaR and ES to replacing Gaussian GBM
with a specified common stochastic variance scenario? The comparison measures
model sensitivity; it does not identify which distribution is more accurate.

## Method

Use the same dated CSV calibration as the existing studio: daily log-return
sample covariance, annualization by 252 and fixed 10% diagonal shrinkage.
The bundled CSV is generated synthetic data with 253 closes and 252 returns,
from 2025-01-13 through the 2025-12-31 cutoff. User CSVs also flow through both
comparisons, with asset-name mapping, cutoff and fingerprint preserved.
The new comparison exports its complete fitted parameter snapshot.

Keep the four European contracts, units [50, -30, 40, 20], strikes
[100, 85, 115, 65], six-month initial maturity, 21/252-year horizon h and
3% rate fixed. Black–Scholes revaluation uses the same fitted historical
volatility in both models, at the initial date and at the horizon. In
particular, the latent variance state does not alter the repricing volatility.
This isolates scenario-distribution sensitivity rather than combining it with
a new option-pricing rule.

Let Z be four independent standard normals, L the Cholesky correlation factor,
and sigma the fitted volatility vector. GBM has V=1. The alternative independently
draws one latent variance per book path:

- P(V=4) = 0.1;
- P(V=2/3) = 0.9.

All assets share V. Conditional on V, terminal asset j is

```text
S_j(h) = S_j(0) exp((r - V sigma_j^2/2) h + sigma_j sqrt(h V) (L Z)_j).
```

Thus E[V]=1 and Cov(sqrt(V) L Z)=L L^T. For a standardized marginal innovation,
E[X^4]=3 E[V^2]=3(0.9(2/3)^2+0.1(4)^2)=6, versus Gaussian kurtosis 3.
These are innovation identities: total log-return covariance also contains the
random conditional-drift term h^2 sigma_j^2 sigma_k^2 Var(V)/4. It is not exactly
the GBM log-return covariance. Integrating each conditional normal gives
E[S_j(h)|V]=S_j(0) exp(rh), so expected terminal spots match GBM exactly in
the model. A finite mixture has larger tails relative to this Gaussian baseline
but is not a power-law distribution.

The state persists over this single horizon. This is a specified two-state
stochastic variance scenario, not a calibrated dynamic volatility process.
The [normal-mixture literature](https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/fin_fac/15/)
provides background on conditional return shape; no replication of Durham's
dynamic model or empirical results is claimed. The 10% probability and variance
multipliers are sensitivity assumptions, not estimates from the CSV or choices
optimized against the results. Common V also changes joint tail dependence;
the experiment does not separately attribute marginal and dependence effects.

Each model uses 32,768 plain Monte Carlo paths at display seed 1729. Twelve
additional pairs use seeds 100–111. Each pair uses exactly the same normal
matrix from default_rng(seed). Regime uniforms use a separate generator from
SeedSequence(seed, spawn_key=(3103,)); no extra normal draw is consumed in the
GBM stream. These are practical pseudorandom streams, not a proof of physical
independence. Original public defaults and the four prior studio cases remain
reproducible. Sobol remains available in the engine; for a mixture it combines
Sobol normal draws with pseudorandom regimes. The recorded mixture study uses
plain Monte Carlo throughout.

Loss is initial value minus discounted future value. VaR uses NumPy's linearly
interpolated empirical 99% quantile; ES averages observations at or above that
threshold, giving 328 tail observations per recorded model run. PFE is the 99%
quantile of positive undiscounted future book value. Each paired difference is
mixture minus GBM. The standard deviation across twelve differences and its
standard error divided by sqrt(12) describe numerical variability only.
This is not a confidence interval for real-world VaR or model error.

The comparison evaluates 26 × 32,768 = 851,968 book paths, 3,407,872 future
option revaluations and 26 initial book valuations. This excludes calibration,
random generation, wall time and quantum-oracle costs. It is additional to
the existing calibration, convergence and bounded quantum experiments.

## Actual findings

The recorded values below are USD, measured on the bundled synthetic input.
Both models have initial book value 141.0792124651 USD.

| Scenario at seed 1729 | 99% VaR | 99% ES | 99% PFE | Mean loss |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Calibrated GBM | 242.9934 | 278.7069 | 376.4965 | 0.7416 |
| Common variance mixture | 271.5146 | 355.3055 | 424.7513 | 0.2593 |
| Mixture minus GBM | +28.5212 | +76.5986 | +48.2548 | -0.4823 |

| Paired difference, twelve seeds | Mean | SD | Numerical SE of mean |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| VaR | +26.5503 | 5.4267 | 1.5666 |
| ES | +81.2760 | 7.0574 | 2.0373 |
| PFE | +38.9282 | 5.0889 | 1.4690 |
| Mean loss | -0.4452 | 0.2272 | 0.0656 |

Both tail-loss metrics increase in the displayed run and across the paired
means, with a larger ES response. Mean loss decreases while tail risk rises:
an expectation is not a substitute for a tail metric. Fixed Black–Scholes
revaluation under mixture scenarios is deliberately not a self-consistent
mixture pricing model; matching expected asset prices does not imply matching
expected option-book values or zero expected discounted book loss.
The comparison does not establish that all portfolios would show this ranking.
The GBM row differs from the calibration case's 243.98 USD VaR because the
latter uses scrambled Sobol instead of this experiment's plain Monte Carlo.

All 20 unit tests pass, including independent formula reconstruction, mixture
moments, sample innovation covariance, paired replay and summary reconstruction,
CSV cutoff and column permutation, invalid models and existing public APIs.
The four prior cases reproduce, and all five current studio cases reproduce.
See [results](../results/latest.json), [studio artifact](../docs/study.json)
and the [validation receipt](QE03-validation.json).

## Limitations

No real market observations, physical probability calibration or held-out
accuracy target are supplied. Historical volatility is a pricing proxy, not
an implied surface. The fixed states do not estimate skewness, transitions,
leverage or serial volatility clustering. The covariance normalization applies
to innovations, not exactly to total log returns. A common variance draw also
changes dependence, so this is a joint model change rather than an attribution
of one marginal tail parameter. Parameter estimation uncertainty, regime-choice
sensitivity, window sensitivity and out-of-sample validation remain unmeasured.
Finite tail samples and twelve replicates do not establish tail coverage.
No quantum estimation of this portfolio risk, market edge, novel algorithm or
hardware advantage is claimed. The existing quantum oracle remains a separate
bounded discrete example without portfolio state-preparation cost accounting.

## What the author should understand for an interview

- Derive E[V], innovation covariance and kurtosis; distinguish these from the
  full log-return covariance and explain the conditional drift correction.
- Explain why fixed repricing inputs isolate scenario sensitivity and why
  this differs from a self-consistent mixture option-pricing model.
- Distinguish a common latent variance from independent per-asset scales and
  explain its effect on joint tails as well as marginal distributions.
- Replay a shared seed and explain how pairing reduces noise in a difference
  without proving either model accurate or guaranteeing variance reduction.
- Interpret ES, VaR and mean loss separately, and distinguish numerical SE,
  parameter uncertainty and model misspecification.
- Describe what dynamic calibration and held-out observations would be needed
  before drawing conclusions about real-world tail accuracy.
